1) Problem: Understanding Coordination

What is wrong with the way we coordinate?

Before we can unlock the capacity of individuals, organisations, tribes, processes and data to operate as a coordinated system, we must first understand what coordination is, how it works, and why the cost of maintaining coordination between entities, increases as a system scales.

If we examine the traditional supply-chain model, we can see that financial value flows from right-to-left, while goods and services flow from left-to-right. Information can therefore be said, to flow in both directions, allowing stakeholders to communicate, coordinate and respond to one another.

However, there is a problem with this model. The stakeholders are structurally interdependent: if one stakeholder is removed, the relationships that allow other stakeholders to perform their functions can be disrupted. For example, if Distribution is removed, the Consumer may no longer be able to access the goods produced upstream; if Production is removed, Suppliers may no longer have a downstream recipient for the raw materials they supply. The issue is therefore not simply the loss of an individual stakeholder, but the break in the dependency network connecting the remaining stakeholders.

So what, you might think, that's normal? Stakeholders come and go, and the gap is simply filled by somebody else?

The problem, however, is deeper. It concerns how stakeholders maintain the relationships through which they coordinate. If we denormalise the model so that all flows are represented in the same direction, we can see that the traditional supply chain has a fundamentally linear dependency structure. Each stakeholder must independently manage the sending and receiving of information required to coordinate with the stakeholders it is connected to. The relationship therefore depends on both sides maintaining their respective communication processes.

When a stakeholder is removed or replaced, its communication relationships cannot simply continue. The new stakeholder must establish and maintain the necessary send-and-receive relationships with surrounding stakeholders, along with the associated agreements, information, policies and operational arrangements. This creates economic, organisational and energy costs that increase as relationships are established, maintained or replaced.

In computer science the study of the sending and receiving of signals is called computer networking.

In computer networking, communication between devices can be understood in terms of how information is permitted to flow between the endpoints of a link. Simplex, half-duplex and full-duplex represent progressively more capable forms of communication, moving from fixed, unidirectional relationships toward bidirectional communication between independently addressable endpoints.

Simplex

In simplex mode, communication is unidirectional, like a one-way street. One device or stakeholder can transmit, while the other can only receive. Because communication only occurs in one direction, the full capacity of the channel can be dedicated to transmitting data in that direction.

This represents a highly constrained relationship: the sender and receiver have predetermined roles and the communication path is established in advance. The diagram describes this as early or physical binding because the relationship is tied to a fixed connection between the endpoints.

Example: A traditional broadcast system, such as a television or radio transmitter, sends information to receivers, but the receivers do not transmit information back through the same communication relationship.

Half-duplex

In half-duplex mode, each endpoint can both transmit and receive, but not simultaneously. When one device is transmitting, the other receives; communication can then reverse direction.

This provides greater flexibility because the roles of sender and receiver are no longer permanently fixed. However, the endpoints still have to coordinate when and how the channel is used. The diagram represents this as late or frequency binding, where the relationship can be established dynamically when two endpoints select or share the same communication frequency or channel.

Example: Walkie-talkies allow both stakeholders to speak and listen, but communication occurs one direction at a time. The stakeholders must coordinate access to the shared channel.

Full-duplex

In full-duplex mode, both endpoints can transmit and receive simultaneously. Communication therefore becomes genuinely bidirectional: each endpoint can send information while simultaneously receiving information from the other.

This is significant from a coordination perspective because communication is no longer constrained to a sequence of alternating transmissions. Both stakeholders can continuously exchange information and respond to changes in the state of the relationship. The communication relationship can therefore be established through logical addressing rather than requiring a permanently fixed physical connection.

Example: Modern computer networks support full-duplex communication between endpoints, allowing data to travel in both directions simultaneously. Logical addressing, such as IP addressing, allows endpoints to identify and communicate with one another across a network without requiring them to be physically bound to one another.

Overlaying the traditional supply-chain model with the three networking models, it most closely resembles a half-duplex coordination system, where each stakeholder independently maintains the policies and relationships required to coordinate with others.

Each stakeholder or service operates as an independent node with its own policy, data and network connections. Coordination therefore requires participants to maintain both a local representation of the rules governing their relationship and a connection through which information can be exchanged. As the network grows, so does the number of policy instances and relationships that must be established, maintained and kept consistent.

This creates a fundamental coordination problem: if one stakeholder changes its policy, others may continue operating under older versions, creating policy divergence and misalignment.

For example, if a bank changes its interest-rate policy without informing its customers, a customer may continue making payments according to the previous rate and subsequently be penalised for failing to meet the new payment amount. Both parties remain connected, but are operating under divergent policies.

If a stakeholder or connection becomes unavailable, dependent relationships must also be reconstructed. Governance is therefore duplicated across the network and coupled to its connections, making coordination increasingly difficult as the number of stakeholders grows.

2) Solution: Decoupling Coordination from Governance

Which network type should we use?

Changing the network from half-duplex to full-duplex transforms the coordination model from a single-stakeholder coordination network into a multi-stakeholder coordination network or forum. Stakeholders or their respective services can both send and receive information, or be both managers and members of the same policy.

(P)olicy is used to scope or govern the behavior of a stakeholder or their service.

(F)orum is used to coordinate the activity of stakeholders or their services (i.e. managers or members).

(S)ervice is the source of the operation, human or non-human (e.g. API, IoT, AI agent etc).

(D)ata captures the information produced by a service. This includes both primary data generated internally by the service and secondary or observations, records, measurements and audit information.

3) Eleutherios Engine: CTM-PFSD

How does CTM-PFSD work?

PFSD is the governance protocol, with an additional learning layer, CTM (Continuous Thought Machine).

CTM uses a Hebbian mechanism (BDH) to form associations between policies and services from real outcomes; once an association proves stable, it is formalized (via LNN-ILP) into a proposed rule for governance to review.

A child policy may add or tighten constraints, but can never remove or relax one it inherits from its parent.

For example, CoordinationPolicy sets ChatService's line limit to 60. TicTacToePolicy, extending CoordinationPolicy, may set its own limit to 50 — tighter, so valid. Setting it to 70 would be rejected outright, before anything changes.

What are Federated Policies?

Federation extends the same restriction pathway across organizational boundaries. A company can create a child policy — the same P → R → P mechanism that lets CoordinationPolicy create TicTacToePolicy — that exposes only specific forums and services, while its full policy stays private. A partner runs that child policy directly: they get exactly what it permits, nothing more, and never see what's behind it. Trust doesn't require transparency — each compiled policy carries a graph_hash, committed to the PIC Chain, that a partner can verify independently of ever seeing the source.

4) Humankind: The Future of Eleutherios

UN SDG-17 Partnerships for the goals

UN SDG 17 calls for strengthened multi-stakeholder partnerships, and for reliable data and accountability to verify that those partnerships are actually delivering. CTM-PFSD's federation model is a direct structural answer to both: it lets independent organizations — governments, NGOs, companies, of any size or resourcing level — coordinate through a shared governed forum without merging systems or exposing internal policy. Each party keeps full authority over what they expose; the PIC Chain gives every partner, funder, and auditor an immutable, independently verifiable record of what was agreed and what actually happened — not a self-reported report, but a provable one.

Example

A health ministry, an international NGO, and a local logistics partner need to coordinate a vaccine distribution program — three organizations, three different internal systems, none willing or able to expose their full operations to the others.

Each keeps their own private policy. Each creates a child policy exposing only what's needed for this partnership — the ministry exposes an allocation-approval forum, the NGO exposes a delivery-tracking service, the logistics partner exposes a fulfillment-confirmation service. None of their internal budgets, procurement rules, or operational policies are visible to the other two.

Every dose allocated, every delivery confirmed, every approval given is recorded permanently in each organization's PIC Chain — so a funder auditing the program six months later isn't relying on quarterly reports assembled after the fact. The record was made the moment each action happened, and it can't be edited retroactively.

Digital Product Policy (DPP)

The Digital Product Policy (DPP) binds a machine readable Policy and a living Forum to each physical product via a scannable digital label (QR/NFC). This lets the product “participate” in governance across its life—manufacture, ownership, repair, reuse, recycling, and end of life—while capturing signed Data events and enabling actionable Services (e.g., repair booking, take back credits, recalls).

5) Support: Scaling Partners

Why become a scaling partner?

Eleutherios is developing CTM-PFSD as an open governance protocol for coordinating human and non-human actors across organisational and technical boundaries.

We have reached the point where the core prototype is working, and the immediate constraint is no longer defining the architecture — it is having sufficient access to frontier AI capability to rigorously develop, verify and test it at the level required for wider adoption.

We are seeking scaling partners who can provide credits, model access or other frontier-AI resources to help us continue this work. Access to models such as Claude and other leading frontier systems would directly accelerate formal verification, governance research, evaluation and development of CTM-PFSD’s learning layer.

This support would help move CTM-PFSD from a working prototype toward independent verification, real-world evaluation and an open, adoptable governance standard.
